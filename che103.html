CHE 103: Chemistry Practical I

Course Code: CHE 103

Basic laboratory techniques, safety procedures, qualitative analysis, titrations, and experimental methods in chemistry

Back to All Courses

1. Laboratory Safety and Equipment

Chemistry laboratories contain potentially hazardous materials and equipment. Understanding and following safety procedures is essential.

General Laboratory Safety Rules:

  • Always wear appropriate personal protective equipment (PPE): lab coat, safety goggles, closed-toe shoes
  • Never work alone in the laboratory
  • Know the location and proper use of safety equipment: eyewash stations, safety showers, fire extinguishers, fire blankets
  • Never eat, drink, or apply cosmetics in the laboratory
  • Keep work areas clean and organized
  • Dispose of chemical waste properly according to instructions
  • Report all accidents, injuries, or spills immediately to the instructor
  • Wash hands thoroughly after handling chemicals and before leaving the laboratory

⚠️ Important Safety Warnings:

  • Never taste or smell chemicals directly
  • Always add acid to water, never water to acid
  • Handle flammable materials away from open flames
  • Use fume hoods when working with volatile or toxic chemicals
  • Know the hazards of the chemicals you're working with (check MSDS - Material Safety Data Sheets)

Common Laboratory Equipment:

Equipment Purpose Special Instructions
Beakers Mixing, heating, and holding liquids Not for accurate measurements
Graduated Cylinder Accurate volume measurements Read at meniscus at eye level
Burette Dispensing precise volumes of liquids (titrations) Rinse with solution before use, avoid air bubbles
Pipette Transferring precise volumes of liquids Use pipette bulb, never mouth pipetting
Analytical Balance Precise mass measurements (to 0.0001g) Calibrate regularly, keep clean, avoid vibrations
Hot Plate Heating solutions without open flame Use with stirring function for even heating
Bunsen Burner Heating with open flame Know different flame types (safety, heating, roaring)
Test Tubes Small-scale reactions and heating Never point open end toward anyone

Glassware Handling and Cleaning:

  • Inspect glassware for cracks or chips before use
  • Clean glassware thoroughly after each use
  • Use appropriate cleaning solutions (detergent, acid baths for stubborn residues)
  • Rinse with distilled water after cleaning
  • Store clean glassware in designated areas

Chemical Hazard Symbols:

Symbol Meaning Examples Precautions
🔥 Flammable Ethanol, acetone, ether Keep away from heat, sparks, open flames
☣️ Toxic Cyanides, mercury salts Use in fume hood, wear gloves, avoid inhalation
⚠️ Corrosive Strong acids (HCl, H₂SO₄), strong bases (NaOH) Wear gloves and goggles, handle with care
💥 Explosive Picric acid, peroxides Handle minimal quantities, store properly
☢️ Radioactive Radioisotopes Use shielding, monitor exposure, special disposal

2. Basic Laboratory Techniques

Mastery of basic laboratory techniques is essential for accurate and reproducible experimental results.

Weighing Techniques:

  • Using Analytical Balance:
    1. Ensure balance is level and calibrated
    2. Tare (zero) the balance with weighing container
    3. Add substance to container using spatula
    4. Close balance doors during weighing for accurate readings
    5. Record mass to appropriate significant figures
  • Weighing by Difference: Weigh container with substance, then weigh container after removing some substance; the difference is the mass transferred
  • Weighing Hygroscopic Substances: Weigh quickly in closed containers to prevent moisture absorption

Volume Measurement Techniques:

  • Using Graduated Cylinder:
    1. Place cylinder on level surface
    2. Pour liquid to desired volume
    3. Read at bottom of meniscus at eye level
    4. Estimate between graduation marks for better precision
  • Using Pipette:
    1. Rinse pipette with solution to be measured
    2. Use pipette bulb to draw liquid above calibration mark
    3. Adjust meniscus to calibration mark by releasing pressure
    4. Deliver to receiving vessel with tip touching wall
    5. Wait for drainage (some pipettes require blowing out last drop)
  • Using Burette:
    1. Rinse burette with solution to be used
    2. Fill burette above zero mark
    3. Open stopcock to fill tip and remove air bubbles
    4. Adjust meniscus to zero or convenient starting point
    5. Read volume at eye level, estimating between marks

Heating Techniques:

  • Bunsen Burner Operation:
    • Check gas hose connections
    • Open gas valve slightly
    • Light with striker away from burner
    • Adjust air intake for desired flame (yellow safety flame for standby, blue heating flame for work)
    • Always turn off gas when not in use
  • Heating Liquids:
    • Use proper container (beaker, flask, test tube)
    • Never heat closed containers
    • Stir liquids during heating to prevent bumping
    • Use boiling chips for even boiling
    • Direct flame at liquid, not container walls
  • Hot Plate Usage: More controlled heating, especially for flammable liquids

Filtration Techniques:

  • Gravity Filtration: Slow but simple, using filter paper in funnel
  • Vacuum Filtration: Faster, using Büchner funnel and vacuum pump
  • Decantation: Pouring off liquid after solid settles
  • Filter Paper Selection: Choose appropriate pore size (qualitative vs. quantitative)

Solution Preparation:

  • Preparing Molar Solutions:
    1. Calculate required mass of solute: mass = M × V × Molar Mass
    2. Weigh solute accurately
    3. Dissolve in less than final volume of solvent
    4. Transfer to volumetric flask
    5. Add solvent to calibration mark (meniscus at mark)
    6. Mix thoroughly by inversion
  • Serial Dilution: Preparing a series of solutions of decreasing concentration
  • Standard Solutions: Solutions of precisely known concentration, used as references

💡 Pro Tips for Accurate Measurements:

  • Always calibrate equipment before use
  • Record measurements immediately to avoid memory errors
  • Use appropriate significant figures based on equipment precision
  • Repeat measurements for better accuracy and precision
  • Keep laboratory notebook neat and organized with all data

3. Qualitative Analysis

Qualitative analysis involves identifying the components of a substance without necessarily determining their quantities.

Preliminary Tests:

  • Physical Examination: Color, state (solid, liquid, gas), crystal structure, odor (waft, don't inhale directly)
  • Solubility Tests: Test solubility in water, acids, bases, organic solvents
  • Flame Tests: Identify metal ions by characteristic flame colors:
    • Na⁺: Bright yellow
    • K⁺: Lilac (violet) through cobalt blue glass
    • Ca²⁺: Brick red
    • Ba²⁺: Apple green
    • Cu²⁺: Blue-green
    • Li⁺: Crimson red

Tests for Cations:

Cation Test Observation
Ammonium (NH₄⁺) Add NaOH, heat gently Pungent smell of NH₃, turns moist red litmus blue
Lead (Pb²⁺) Add KI solution Yellow precipitate of PbI₂
Silver (Ag⁺) Add HCl or NaCl solution White precipitate of AgCl, soluble in NH₃
Copper (Cu²⁺) Add NH₃ solution Blue precipitate, then deep blue solution
Iron(II) (Fe²⁺) Add potassium ferricyanide K₃[Fe(CN)₆] Dark blue precipitate (Turnbull's blue)
Iron(III) (Fe³⁺) Add potassium ferrocyanide K₄[Fe(CN)₆] Deep blue precipitate (Prussian blue)
Aluminum (Al³⁺) Add NaOH, then excess White gelatinous precipitate, soluble in excess NaOH
Zinc (Zn²⁺) Add NaOH, then excess White precipitate, soluble in excess NaOH

Tests for Anions:

Anion Test Observation
Carbonate (CO₃²⁻) Add dilute acid Effervescence (CO₂ gas), turns limewater milky
Sulfate (SO₄²⁻) Add BaCl₂ solution White precipitate of BaSO₄, insoluble in acids
Chloride (Cl⁻) Add AgNO₃, then dilute HNO₃ White precipitate of AgCl, insoluble in HNO₃
Nitrate (NO₃⁻) Brown ring test (FeSO₄ + H₂SO₄) Brown ring at interface
Sulfide (S²⁻) Add dilute acid Rotten egg smell of H₂S
Phosphate (PO₄³⁻) Add ammonium molybdate + HNO₃, heat Yellow precipitate of ammonium phosphomolybdate

Systematic Analysis Scheme:

  1. Group I Cations (Ag⁺, Pb²⁺, Hg₂²⁺): Precipitated as chlorides by adding dilute HCl
  2. Group II Cations (Hg²⁺, Pb²⁺, Bi³⁺, Cu²⁺, Cd²⁺): Precipitated as sulfides in acidic medium by H₂S
  3. Group III Cations (Al³⁺, Cr³⁺, Fe³⁺): Precipitated as hydroxides by NH₄OH in presence of NH₄Cl
  4. Group IV Cations (Zn²⁺, Mn²⁺, Ni²⁺, Co²⁺): Precipitated as sulfides in alkaline medium by H₂S
  5. Group V Cations (Ba²⁺, Sr²⁺, Ca²⁺): Precipitated as carbonates by (NH₄)₂CO₃ in presence of NH₄Cl and NH₄OH
  6. Group VI Cations (Mg²⁺, K⁺, Na⁺, NH₄⁺): Remain in solution, tested individually

Qualitative Analysis Procedure:

  1. Prepare original solution (O.S.) of unknown sample
  2. Perform preliminary tests (physical, flame, solubility)
  3. Test for anions in separate portions of O.S.
  4. Systematically test for cations using group reagents
  5. Confirm each ion with specific tests
  6. Record all observations carefully in lab notebook
  7. Interpret results to identify compounds present

4. Titration Techniques

Titration is a quantitative analytical technique used to determine the concentration of an unknown solution by reacting it with a solution of known concentration.

Types of Titrations:

Type Principle Indicator Example
Acid-Base Neutralization reaction Phenolphthalein, methyl orange HCl vs NaOH
Redox Electron transfer Self-indicating or starch (iodine) KMnO₄ vs Fe²⁺
Complexometric Complex formation Eriochrome Black T, murexide EDTA vs Ca²⁺
Precipitation Formation of precipitate Chromate (Mohr), adsorption AgNO₃ vs NaCl

Acid-Base Titration Procedure (HCl vs NaOH):

  1. Preparation:
    • Clean and rinse all glassware (burette, pipette, conical flask)
    • Prepare standard NaOH solution (primary standard: potassium hydrogen phthalate)
    • Prepare HCl solution of approximately known concentration
    • Add 2-3 drops of phenolphthalein indicator to acid solution
  2. Titration:
    1. Fill burette with NaOH solution, record initial reading
    2. Pipette 25.0 mL of HCl solution into clean conical flask
    3. Add NaOH from burette with continuous swirling
    4. Slow down addition near endpoint (color change persists longer)
    5. Stop when faint pink color persists for 30 seconds
    6. Record final burette reading
  3. Calculations:
    • Volume of NaOH used = Final reading - Initial reading
    • M₁V₁ (NaOH) = M₂V₂ (HCl) at equivalence point
    • Calculate concentration of HCl: M₂ = (M₁V₁)/V₂
    • Repeat titration until consistent results (within 0.1 mL)

Redox Titration (KMnO₄ vs Fe²⁺):

  1. Preparation:
    • Prepare standard KMnO₄ solution (needs standardization with oxalic acid)
    • Prepare Fe²⁺ solution (usually FeSO₄ or (NH₄)₂Fe(SO₄)₂)
    • Add dilute H₂SO₄ to provide acidic medium
    • KMnO₄ is self-indicating (purple to colorless to faint pink)
  2. Titration:
    1. Fill burette with KMnO₄ solution
    2. Pipette Fe²⁺ solution into conical flask, add H₂SO₄
    3. Titrate with KMnO₄ until permanent faint pink color
    4. Initial slow addition, then faster as reaction proceeds
    5. Warm solution if reaction is slow at room temperature
  3. Calculations:
    • 5Fe²⁺ + MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ → 5Fe³⁺ + Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O
    • Mole ratio: 5 moles Fe²⁺ : 1 mole MnO₄⁻
    • Calculate Fe²⁺ concentration from KMnO₄ volume and concentration

Titration Errors and Precautions:

  • Systematic Errors:
    • Incorrect calibration of glassware
    • Impure reagents or standards
    • Incorrect indicator choice or amount
  • Random Errors:
    • Parallax error in reading burette
    • Inconsistent endpoint detection
    • Spills or splashes
  • Precautions:
    • Rinse glassware with appropriate solutions
    • Remove air bubbles from burette tip
    • Read burette at eye level, estimate between marks
    • Perform multiple titrations for accuracy
    • Discard first titration (often less accurate)

💡 Titration Endpoint Tips:

  • For acid-base titrations, choose indicator with pKa near pH at equivalence point
  • Practice detecting color changes with known solutions first
  • Use white background to better observe color changes
  • Add last drops slowly (dropwise) near endpoint
  • Record volumes to 2 decimal places (0.01 mL precision for burette)

5. Physical Chemistry Experiments

Physical chemistry experiments involve measurements of physical properties and their relationship to chemical composition and structure.

Determination of Melting Point:

  • Purpose: Identify compounds and check purity
  • Apparatus: Melting point apparatus, capillary tubes, thermometer
  • Procedure:
    1. Pack sample tightly in capillary tube (2-3 mm height)
    2. Attach to thermometer with rubber band
    3. Heat slowly (1-2°C/min) near expected melting point
    4. Record temperature when sample begins to melt and when completely melted
    5. Report as melting range
  • Interpretation: Pure compounds have sharp melting point (1-2°C range); impurities lower and broaden melting range

Determination of Boiling Point:

  • Purpose: Identify liquids and check purity
  • Apparatus: Distillation setup, thermometer, heating mantle
  • Procedure:
    1. Set up simple distillation apparatus
    2. Add liquid to distillation flask with boiling chips
    3. Heat until steady distillation
    4. Record temperature when distillate begins to collect
    5. Continue recording as distillation proceeds
  • Interpretation: Pure liquids have constant boiling point; mixtures distill over temperature range

pH Measurement and Buffer Preparation:

  • pH Meter Calibration:
    1. Use standard buffer solutions (pH 4, 7, 10)
    2. Rinse electrode with distilled water between measurements
    3. Calibrate using 2-point or 3-point calibration
  • Buffer Preparation (Acetate Buffer pH 4.76):
    1. Calculate amounts of acetic acid and sodium acetate
    2. Mix calculated volumes/concentrations
    3. Measure pH with calibrated pH meter
    4. Adjust with acid or base if necessary
  • Buffer Capacity: Test by adding small amounts of acid/base and measuring pH change

Determination of Density:

  • Liquid Density:
    1. Weigh empty, dry pycnometer
    2. Fill with liquid, weigh again
    3. Calculate mass of liquid
    4. Density = mass/volume (know pycnometer volume)
  • Solid Density:
    1. Weigh solid
    2. Determine volume by displacement (graduated cylinder with water)
    3. Density = mass/volume
  • Applications: Identify substances, determine concentration, check purity

Colorimetry and Beer-Lambert Law:

  • Principle: A = εlc, where A = absorbance, ε = molar absorptivity, l = path length, c = concentration
  • Procedure:
    1. Prepare standard solutions of known concentrations
    2. Measure absorbance at λmax using spectrophotometer
    3. Plot calibration curve (Absorbance vs Concentration)
    4. Measure absorbance of unknown, read concentration from curve
  • Precautions:
    • Use clean, matched cuvettes
    • Zero instrument with blank (solvent only)
    • Work within linear range of Beer's Law
    • Avoid fingerprints on cuvette optical surfaces

Data Recording and Report Writing:

  • Laboratory Notebook:
    • Record date, experiment title, and objectives
    • Write procedure in your own words
    • Record all observations and measurements immediately
    • Include calculations with proper units and significant figures
    • Draw apparatus diagrams when helpful
  • Laboratory Report:
    • Title, name, date, course information
    • Abstract/Summary
    • Introduction with theory
    • Experimental procedure
    • Results with tables, graphs, calculations
    • Discussion of results, errors, and improvements
    • Conclusion
    • References
